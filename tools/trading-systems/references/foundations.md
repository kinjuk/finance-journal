## 1. Is price predictable?
- Random walk is the null hypothesis; two practical arguments against it: (a) decades of audited systematic results (arbitrage, hedge funds, CTAs) exist, though many technical programs fail; (b) prices move on ANTICIPATION (expect the next rate cut), then react to news relative to expectations; the result can look random while not being so.
- Apparent randomness depends on time interval and data frequency: daily data has more noise; weekly/monthly smoothing shows trends, seasonality, cycles and higher serial correlation. Prices are skewed and have fat tails (longer runs than a normal distribution). Fat tails are the reason trend methods work; the extra tail mass comes from fewer short runs.
- Non-random drivers: fund flows/redemptions regardless of timing, policy-driven long trends, anticipation-driven short moves, extremes far from value (mean reversion), stress periods.
- Fundamentals drive price but are too late/unreliable to trade directly (data revisions, conflicts of interest); technical methods are an objective way to read them. Central banks watch markets; a currency rising fast is not stoppable until exhausted.
- Equilibrium: stocks, return balanced against risk-free; futures, supply and demand.

## 2. Noise, data frequency and style
- Pick the data frequency that matches the strategy: long-term macro trend followers want low-frequency (daily/weekly) data; short-term traders (mean reversion, fast directional) want higher-frequency (15-60 min). The shorter the bar, the noisier the chart.
- Efficiency Ratio (ER, "fractal efficiency") = |P_t - P_(t-n)| / sum over i of |P_i - P_(i-1)|. Net move divided by total path. ER near 1 = clean trend, near 0 = noise. Removes volatility (same volatility, larger net move = less noise). Preferred over price density = sum(H-L) / (n-day high - n-day low) and fractal dimension FD = 1 + (ln L + ln 2) / ln(2n) (L = sum of normalized path + range term).
- Finding: 40-day MA trend system (always in market, no costs), Jan 2000-Mar 2012 across many futures/world indices: profit factor rose with ER. Trendiest = short-term interest rates (tied to central-bank policy), then long rates, USD crosses, energy, metals; noisiest and worst for trend = equity indices. Low noise favors trend following; high noise favors mean reversion. The relationship persisted across eras (1990s trended more).
- Implication for design: measure ER by market/timeframe BEFORE choosing trend vs reversion. Use scripts/metrics.py `efficiency_ratio()`.

## 3. Research guidelines (eight)
1. Know what you want and base it on a sound premise (cannot be discovered by testing everything). 2. State the premise in simplest form (Occam). 3. Assume nothing; prove it. 4. Test simplest, most important parts first; build slowly; keep it transparent. 5. Watch for errors of omission (costs, risk). 6. Question good results as hard as bad. 7. No shortcuts; verify others' work and your own spreadsheet formulas. 8. Start at the end: define the goal, work backward, use only necessary inputs.

## 4. Profile of a system (choices that must be explicit)
- Longevity: markets change (technology, participation raising noise especially in index markets, globalization, falling costs which helped fast traders). Prefer methods that adapt; changes are gradual with occasional shocks.
- Data: price and volume, plus sector peers, yield curve, economic data (more robust but less convenient/timely).
- Diversification reduces return and risk; a specialist in one market or two uncorrelated strategies on one market can beat a generic approach.
- Trade selection: filters (confirmation, per-trade risk limit, outside info, volume) add realism but too many cause overfitting or no trades.
- Testing validates; robust on similar markets will never look as good as a single-stock optimum.
- Risk control has tiers: trade, sector, single-system portfolio, multi-system portfolio; volatility-based management beats ad hoc; futures need leverage control. Almost any system can be profitable with risk control and any can ruin without.
- Costs: commissions + slippage; fast systems most sensitive; directional (trend) strategies suffer more slippage than mean reversion; overstating costs rejects good systems.
- Monitoring and feedback: compare live to expected; poor starts can be corrected by monitoring.


## 5. Ch. 2: basic calculations (use these definitions consistently)
- Law of averages: a long losing or winning run is not "due" to reverse; large samples are dominated by typical values. Overbought does not imply "next oversold".
- All testing is overfitting to some degree; in/out-of-sample discipline (see testing.md) is the only mitigation.
- Smaller samples are less reliable (trade counts, years); use distribution measures to put confidence levels on outcomes. Frequency distribution = histogram (bins = (high - low)/(bins-1)); commodity prices spend most time near the supply-demand equilibrium with a short upper tail.
- Returns: standard r = P1/P0 - 1; ln method r = ln(Pt/Pt-1). They differ (standard average 3.3% higher over one month in the book's example); pick one and state it. NAV_t = NAV_(t-1) x (1 + r_t), NAV_0 = 100. Kaufman uses compounded standard returns.
- Annualizing: use 252 trading days (US; 262 Europe; 360 for government/90-day rates; 365 for calendar-day data). AROR (compounded) = (E_n / E_0)^(252/n) - 1. Simple AROR = (E_n/E_0 - 1) x 252/n. ln method: sum(r)/years.
- Volatility = standard deviation of returns; ANNUALIZE BY MULTIPLYING BY SQRT(252) (daily), sqrt(12) (monthly), sqrt(4) quarterly. NOTE: the book's text in one place says "multiply by 252"; the correct and intended scaling is the square root (his own example: daily 1.512% -> 23.8% annualized = 1.512 x 15.87). 1 sigma annual = ~16% chance of losing that much in a year.
- Event risk (price shocks, catastrophic loss) and self-induced overleverage are the two extreme risks.
- Beta = cov(r_A, r_B) / var(r_B) (slope of regression vs the index); alpha = intercept. 0<beta<1 less volatile than index; negative beta moves opposite.
- Adjust to target volatility by scaling the investment (or position size) by actual vol / target vol: 23.8% actual vs 12% target = invest 1.98x as much capital (deleverage). Kaufman reports all results at 12% target volatility (modest; hedge funds go to 18%). Do the same so systems compare fairly.
- Monthly returns look less volatile than daily (S&P 1990-2010: 18.6% daily-based vs 15.3% monthly) because extremes rarely fall on month-end. Due diligence should demand DAILY data to see mid-month drawdowns. Return is unchanged.
- Downside risk: semivariance = sum of (shortfalls below mean or target)^2 / n (lower partial moment). Practical version: daily drawdown from peak equity; only days below peak count; use standard deviation or sum of squares. Weakness: discards upside-risk relation; with little data use full distribution.
- Standard measures: information ratio = AROR / annualized risk; Sharpe subtracts risk-free; Treynor = (AROR - rf)/beta; max drawdown (percentage from highest NAV to lowest subsequent NAV) because one big drawdown disappears inside a standard deviation; Calmar = AROR / max drawdown; Sortino uses downside volatility (or stdev of below-peak days). Most popular ratios rank candidates similarly; simplest is best. Optimum = higher return at same risk or lower risk at same return (efficient frontier).

## 6. Ch. 3: Charting (what is usable for systems)
- Charts reflect crowd behavior, so patterns are partly self-fulfilling: novices sell double tops, buy breakouts. Know the popular ones defensively. Interpretation is subjective (data frequency, start date, horizon all change the pattern), so any rule must be coded precisely before it can be tested.
- Behavioral cause of patterns: orders cluster at round numbers (26.00 > 26.50/26.25 > others); S&P at 1310.00 vs 1306.50; support/resistance levels held predictively for ~5 days after being noted (Osler/NY Fed intraday FX study, six firms, three years); the public tends to enter bull markets late (V-tops after publicity: 1974 commodities, 2000 NASDAQ).
- Trendline: up-trendline across lows, down across highs; more touch points = stronger; redraw as the pattern develops; trend changes take priority over profit targets. Horizontal support/resistance from sideways ranges; resistance is less clean than support (more volatility). A bar that pokes through and CLOSES back inside = failed breakout, confirms the old range; do not redraw the line to the failed extreme.
- Major vs minor: longer-interval formations (weekly trendlines, all-time highs, head-and-shoulders) matter more; obvious levels draw the most orders.
- Noise rule: volatility below normal noise is inactivity; returning to normal is NOT a breakout. A triangle whose apex is smaller than normal noise resolves into a flag/pennant and is unreliable. Index markets have the most noise (participation, anticipation); short rates the least (tied to central bank).
- Tops/bottoms in increasing complexity: V, double/triple, rounded, broadening, head-and-shoulders, complex. V-tops are easy after the fact, hard to anticipate. Accumulation (low) / distribution (high) are long phases.
- Price objectives: most reliable = prior major support/resistance (set the target just before the level). Formation-based targets scale with the pattern's own volatility, measured from the breakout point (e.g. rectangle: breakout level + range height). Take profits early if volume fades or pattern stalls ("close enough"); targets are guidelines. Use multiple targets (3-5) to scale out and reduce risk; re-enter on a new breakout.
- Practical: trends are easier in hindsight; a new trend signal always arrives at maximum uncertainty. Success needs only ~30-35% winners for trend systems because wins are much larger. Long-term trends (policy-driven, e.g. weekly 10-year note) are more reliable than short-term; the daily drift of a long-term trend (e.g. 2% yield fall ~ 0.064%/day) is swamped by daily noise, so use weekly/lower-frequency data to see it. Small trendline penetrations followed by a return confirm the trend; do not redraw. Confirm with simultaneous short- and long-term breakouts.
- Evolution: index futures/ETFs plus program arbitrage force component stocks to move together (index levels now act as support/resistance for individual stocks); sector diversification weakens. Asian equity indices, once volatility-adjusted and rebased to 100, look almost identical: money flow beats fundamentals, so correlation risk is higher than it looks. Use rebased, volatility-adjusted series to compare markets.


## 7. Ch. 4: Charting systems and techniques
- Dunnigan (1950s): thrust method on swing charts. Downswing = today's high AND low both below those of the swing's highest day; inside/outside days do not change direction; swing top/bottom = highest high of upswing / lowest low of downswing. Lesson he learned the hard way: each market needs its own swing size (a fixed $ or % swing fails across markets; breakouts alone produced long losing strings). Five buy patterns: test of bottom (within a set % of prior low), closing-price reversal (new swing low then higher close), narrow range (< half the swing's largest range), inside range, penetration of the top. Reverse for sells.
- Pivot point = highest high or lowest low in the center of 1-3 bars each side (needs NO minimum retracement, but confirmation lags). Best use: trend following, buy on break above the previous pivot high, sell on break below the previous pivot low. Using 10-20 bars each side makes it behave like a macro-trend system.
- Retracement ratios folklore: 50% (Gann, widely cited), 1/3-1/2 (Schabacker), 25% (Angas), 1/2, 2/3, 3/4 (Dunnigan/Tubbs), 0.382/0.618 (Fibonacci, Elliott), 100% (return to start). No test in the book proves a ratio; treat as self-fulfilling support/resistance levels and verify before use.
- Channel breakout: (1) swing method: trendline through the two latest swing lows (or regression through the last 3-5 swing points), parallel through the highest high; (2) straight-line fit: regress close on 1..t since the last swing, take the largest deviations below (BL) and above (BU) to set support and resistance. Moving channel: M_t = avg of (H+L+C)/3 over n days; R_t = avg(H-L) over n; bands U = M_t + (M_t - M_(t-1)) + f x R_t, L = M_t + (M_t - M_(t-1)) - f x R_t; long when next price > U, short when < L; profit objective = one band width beyond the breakout, recomputed daily as volatility changes. Alternative: regression slope + standard deviation of price changes (see Bollinger bands, trend.md/Ch. 8 notes).
- CCI = (Typical price - SMA of typical price)/(0.015 x mean deviation); TP = (H+L+C)/3. Mean-reversion tool; works as a standard-deviation channel. Overbought can persist for weeks: simple overbought/oversold rules give frequent small gains and an occasional very large loss (needs a stop or trend filter).
- Computer recognition of patterns (Lo, Mamaysky, Wang 2000): smooth with kernel regression, define 10 formations from the last 5 local extrema E1..E5 over a rolling 38-day window (e.g. head-and-shoulders: E3 > E1,E5; E1,E5 within 1.5% of their average; E2,E4 within 1.5%; double top: E1 and Eb within 1.5%, separated by > 22 days; rectangle: tops and bottoms within 0.75%). Tested on hundreds of US stocks 1962-96: double tops/bottoms >2,000 occurrences each, head-and-shoulders >1,600; random series had only 1/3 as many H&S, so patterns are not random. 3-day forward returns were positive for all but the triangular top; statistically significant: H&S top, broadening bottom, rectangle top and bottom, double top. Rising volume generally improved results. Note the 3-day horizon and no cost model: this is evidence of non-randomness, not a tradable system.
- Bulkowski ranking (Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns): best bullish = upward breakout of rectangle top, falling wedge, ascending triangle, double bottom, symmetric triangle after a decline; best bearish = descending scallops, downward breakout of symmetric triangle in a downtrend, broadening tops/bottoms. Symmetric triangle is the most dependable (reliable whichever way it breaks).
