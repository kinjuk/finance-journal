## 1. Required vs expected return
- Required return is what compensates for risk; expected return is what you earn at the price you pay given your forecasts. A buy requires expected return > required return. Value and price are the same thing only when they agree.
- Required return is estimated, not observed: CAPM or a multi-factor model gives a starting point; treat it as a range. Valuation errors from r are as large as errors from forecasts, so always sensitivity-test.
- Active investing: the question is not "what is the value" but "what is the expected return at this price, and does it exceed what the risk requires".

## 2. Nature of risk
- Return distributions: realized returns are noisy; risk is about the dispersion of possible outcomes, not the past variance alone. Diversification removes idiosyncratic risk; asset pricing models price the remaining (systematic) risk.
- Pricing models depend on assumptions you cannot verify; use them as inputs, not as truth, and say how much the answer moves if beta, risk premium or risk-free rate shifts.

## 3. Fundamental risk analysis
Risk sits in the operating drivers, so analyze it there:
- Risk to ROCE: variability of RNOA (margin and turnover) and of financing costs, operating leverage, financial leverage (FLEV), operating liability leverage. High fixed costs and high debt increase earnings variance.
- Growth risk: uncertainty in sales and NOA growth; growth that requires heavy investment is riskier than growth from efficiency.
- Value-at-risk profile: build scenarios for the key drivers (sales growth, PM, ATO, long-run fade), compute value under each, and present the distribution (downside, base, upside) and probability-weighted value. Include the cost of "what if the competitive advantage ends early".
- Options: adaptation options (ability to redeploy assets, cut costs, change strategy) reduce downside; growth options (the right to invest if conditions turn favourable) add upside value beyond the base pro forma. Count them separately and conservatively.

## 4. Reverse engineering expected returns
Take the market price and your forecasts; solve for the discount rate that equates PV of forecast residual income (or FCF) to price. Compare to the required return from the model. If the expected return exceeds required by a wide margin, either the forecasts are too optimistic, the required return is too low, or the stock is underpriced. Investigate which before acting.

## 5. Credit analysis
Credit analysis asks whether the firm can meet obligations, not what equity is worth.
- Ratio analysis (use reformulated statements): current ratio, quick ratio, cash flow from operations to current liabilities, capex relative to CFO, total debt to total assets and to equity, long-term debt ratio, interest coverage (earnings basis: OI/net interest; cash basis: unlevered CFO/net cash interest), CFO to total debt.
- Credit scoring models combine such ratios, but are backward-looking; use as screens.
- Full-information forecasting: forecast operating income, NOA and FCF as in valuation, then test whether cash available for debt service covers the debt service requirement, year by year, including downside scenarios. Cash available for debt service = FCF - net dividends (= OI - change in NOA - net dividends). Debt service requirement = required interest and preferred dividends + required principal repayments + lease payments.
- Covenant and refinancing risk: map maturities against forecast free cash flow; flag years with large refinancing needs.

## 6. Liquidity planning and financial strategy
- Treasurer's rule: free cash flow after interest vs dividends decides borrowing or lending. Persistent surplus without a plan signals unrecognized investment or payout capacity; persistent deficit signals dependence on capital markets.
- For bond investors: compare yield to the default-risk-adjusted expected return, using forecast coverage under stress.
