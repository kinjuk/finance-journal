## 1. Profitability analysis (three levels)
Level 1 - split ROCE: ROCE = RNOA + FLEV x (RNOA - NBC). Ask: is profitability coming from operations or from leverage? Leverage amplifies but does not create value; a ROCE that is high only because FLEV is high is fragile.
Level 2 - split RNOA: RNOA = PM x ATO. Margin-driven firms (brands) and turnover-driven firms (retailers) behave differently. Compare both levers with history and peers.
Level 3 - drivers of PM (gross margin and each expense ratio) and of ATO (each asset and liability turnover: receivables, inventory, PPE, payables). Also borrowing cost drivers: rates on each financing source.
Also look at: operating liability leverage (supplier financing inflates RNOA relative to ROOA), minority interest sharing ratio, and days ratios (receivables, inventory, payables).

## 2. Core vs unusual items
- Core OI from sales is what the business sustainably earns from operating. Strip unusual items (restructuring, impairments, disposal gains, litigation, one-off tax effects), taxing them at the marginal rate.
- Core other OI: recurring non-sales operating income (equity-method income, royalties) kept in forecasts if persistent.
- Forecast from core. Treat unusual items as zero in forecasts unless they recur (recurring "one-offs" are core costs).
- Decompose year-over-year change in RNOA into: core margin change, turnover change, other core income, unusual items. Persistent margin and turnover changes carry forward; unusual items do not.
- Same logic on financing: core net financial expense vs unusual financial items.

## 3. Growth analysis
- Growth in OI comes from growth in NOA (investment) at constant RNOA, or from improved RNOA. Only the second is value-adding beyond the required return; growth in NOA at RNOA = r adds no value.
- Sustainable growth: CSE grows at ROCE + net investment rate; with no net share issues that is ROCE x retention. Compare forecast growth with what ROCE and payout can finance.
- Operating leverage: fixed costs make OI grow faster than sales when sales grow, and fall faster when they shrink. Estimate the contribution margin; use it to check margin forecasts against sales scenarios.
- Distinguish growth from investment (needs cash, shrinks FCF) from growth from efficiency (adds FCF).

## 4. Simple forecasting (mature, stable firms)
Anchor on the financial statements and add as little speculation as possible.
- No-growth forecast: OI_1 = current core OI.
- Growth forecast: OI_1 = NOA_0 x current core RNOA; NOA and ReOI grow at the current NOA growth rate (or at sales growth if ATO and PM are stable). Value via the simple growth formulas in formulas.md.
- If current RNOA is above the required return, no-growth is conservative and the growth version can be optimistic. Report both as a bracket.
- Adjust toward reality: use the weighted growth (0.7 current + 0.3 long-run) or the two-stage AOIG model with a short-term growth rate and a long-run rate.
- Simple valuation works for firms with steady margin, turnover and sales growth. It fails for start-ups, firms changing business model, or cyclical firms at peak or trough.

## 5. Full forecasting template
Steps (from the pro forma method). Steps 1-7 are enough to value; the rest complete the statements.
1. Forecast sales (volume x price, by segment). Ground it in the market, competitors, marketing plan, capacity.
2. Forecast core profit margin: gross margin and each expense ratio. Include operating leverage.
3. Forecast other core operating income and any unusual items (usually zero).
4. Calculate OI = sales x PM (+ other).
5. Forecast asset turnover by item (receivables, inventory, PPE, payables, other); calculate NOA = sales / ATO, built from line items. Include required capacity investment (plants cost money and may sit idle first).
6. Calculate free cash flow = OI - change in NOA.
7. Choose the cost of capital for operations (and allow changes over time if leverage or rates will change).
8. Forecast net dividends or the target leverage policy.
9. Forecast borrowing cost (after tax) and any change in cost of debt.
10. Compute NFE = after-tax borrowing cost x beginning NFO (or NFA with the asset return).
11. Compute change in NFO = NFE - FCF + net dividends (treasurer's rule).
12. Compute comprehensive income = OI - NFE.
13. Compute CSE = NOA - NFO; confirm it equals prior CSE + CI - net dividends.
14. Adjust value for stock option overhang.
15. Adjust for minority interest (subtract minority earnings x intrinsic P/E, or minority book value x P/B).
Add a terminal growth or fade assumption after the explicit horizon (typically 3-7 years, longer for firms with a clear durable advantage).

## 6. Fade and competitive advantage
- High RNOA and high growth tend to fade as competition arrives. Model fade explicitly: RNOA converging toward the cost of capital or an industry level over the horizon, growth converging to long-run nominal growth.
- Longer, slower fade needs a reason: brand, cost advantage, network effects, regulation. State the reason and its source. Without a reason, fade faster.
- A pro forma that forecasts persistent financial asset buildup is incomplete: ask what happens to the cash. Either it is paid out (set payout higher) or the strategy implies investment you have not forecast.

## 7. Sanity tests on a pro forma
- Compare forecast PM, ATO, RNOA, growth to history and industry; justify deviations.
- Common-size the forecast statements; check each ratio is plausible.
- Verify articulation (CSE two ways, FCF two ways).
- Check implied P/B and P/E at forecast horizon against normal levels.
- Run the three valuation models (ReOI, AOIG, DCF) and confirm agreement.
