# Accounting quality analysis

Forecasts anchor on reported numbers, so check them. Earnings management shifts income between periods; it rarely changes lifetime income, so the useful question is: is current operating income a good base for forecasting?

## 1. Accounting effects on value
- Residual income valuation is robust to the accounting method *if* the method is applied consistently and the forecast reflects it: the book value and residual income move in offsetting ways (balance-sheet value recognized earlier means lower later income).
- Conservative accounting (expensing R&D and advertising, fast depreciation, LIFO in inflation) lowers book value now, raises RNOA, and tends to produce high P/B and sometimes high P/E for growing firms; hidden reserves can later be released to create earnings.
- Liberal accounting (capitalizing costs, slow depreciation, aggressive revenue) does the reverse and then reverses itself with lower future income.
- Growth plus conservative accounting boosts reported income growth and RNOA; growth plus liberal accounting depresses subsequent margins. A forecast horizon too short to see the reversal will misvalue the firm.
- Neutral accounting: book value equals value, so P/B = 1 and RNOA equals the cost of capital in a competitive steady state.
- Hidden reserves and "cookie-jar" accruals allow income smoothing; a sudden reserve release inflates a year's income and should be excluded from core.

## 2. Prelude
Before testing, understand four things: the business and what normal accounting looks like in it (normal bad-debt rates, asset lives); the firm's stated accounting policies (first footnote) and whether they differ from industry norms; where in this business the accounting is most doubtful; and where management is most tempted (covenants, bonus targets, financing events, missed targets, restatements).

## 3. Where manipulation shows up
Focus on changes in NOA, because earnings manipulation moves income by moving assets or liabilities.
- **Capitalization:** costs that should be expensed pushed to the balance sheet (repairs into PPE, promotion and customer acquisition into assets, prepaid expenses, overhead into inventory, capitalized software).
- **Accruals/estimates:** allowances for bad debts and returns, warranty, deferred revenue, restructuring, pension assumptions, lease residual values, inventory write-downs, asset lives.
- **Revenue timing:** booking revenue ahead of delivery, bill-and-hold, channel stuffing, multiple-deliverable contracts, long-term contracts.
- **Classification:** moving operating costs into non-operating or unusual items to lift core income.
- Reverse direction (big baths, excess reserves) also occurs after bad news or before a management change.

## 4. Diagnostics
Pick those matching the business; compare to prior years and peers.
Revenue
- Net sales / cash collected from customers: rising gap is a warning.
- Net sales / net receivables (and days sales outstanding).
- Allowance for returns and discounts relative to sales; bad-debt expense vs actual credit losses; bad-debt reserve / gross receivables.
- Deferred revenue trends vs sales growth.
Expenses and balance sheet
- Depreciation / capex and depreciation / gross PPE (lengthening lives).
- Inventory growth vs sales growth; days inventory; write-downs.
- Warranty expense vs actual warranty claims; accrued liabilities vs sales.
- Advertising, R&D and other discretionary spending / sales (cutting to hit earnings).
- Pension expense and assumptions; restructuring reserves and their later releases.
Cash flow
- Operating income / CFO, and growth of each: income persistently above cash flow suggests accruals are propping up earnings.
- Accruals relative to average NOA.
Other
- Frequent "unusual" or "nonrecurring" items.
- Gains from asset sales regularly offsetting weak operations.
- Auditor changes, late filings, restatements, thin disclosures.

## 5. Normalizing operating income
- Normalized OI = free cash flow + change in normalized NOA, where NOA is restated for what a consistent accounting policy would carry (e.g. capitalized costs reversed, reserves restored to normal levels).
- Practical adjustments: reverse suspect capitalization and add back to expense; restore bad-debt/warranty/restructuring accruals to normal rates; recompute depreciation on a peer-consistent life; remove reserve releases; treat recurring "unusual" costs as core.
- Adjusted EBITDA and EBIT are frequently promoted by management; recompute from the reformulated statements instead.
- Re-run RNOA and the forecast from normalized numbers. State the effect on value.

## 6. Quality scoring and red flags
- Score each diagnostic as better-than-benchmark / in-line / worse, then aggregate into an overall quality view (e.g. high / medium / low). A low-quality score argues for a lower anchor, a larger margin of safety, or avoiding the stock.
- Red flags, financial: income growing faster than cash flow, receivables or inventory outrunning sales, depreciation lives stretching, growing "other" assets, aggressive capitalization, declining accruals reserves, heavy reliance on one-time gains.
- Red flags, institutional: weak audit committee or governance, aggressive management compensation tied to short-term earnings, pending financing or acquisition, covenant pressure, history of SEC issues, complex structures and related parties.
- Quality analysis also identifies possible *positive* distortions (conservative accounting hiding value), which the market may underprice.

## 7. Disclosure quality
Thin, shifting, or boilerplate disclosure is itself a signal. Check changes in policy footnotes, segment disclosures, off-balance-sheet items (leases, guarantees, special-purpose entities), and consistency between management commentary and the statements.
